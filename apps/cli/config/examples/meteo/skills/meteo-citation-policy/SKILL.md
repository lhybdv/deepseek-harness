# Citing professional sources

## When a citation is required

Cite whenever the answer states something a reader could not derive from the
numbers alone:

- A judgement criterion — why this condition counts as suitable or risky.
- A recommended practice — what to do, when, and in what order.
- A hazard characteristic — how a disaster typically develops, or what it damages.
- A qualification on the data — a known limitation of the measure or the period.

Do **not** cite the tool's own arithmetic. A value the observation or forecast
tool returned, and a verdict the rule engine produced, are already attributable to
those tools by name.

## What a citation must identify

A citation is only useful if a reader can reach the same text. Every citation
names the document and the exact chunk, and quotes enough of the passage that the
claim can be checked against the words rather than against a summary of them. A
citation that points at a whole document, or that paraphrases without quoting,
does not let the reader verify anything.

## Rules

1. **Attach the citation to the sentence it supports**, not to a bibliography at
   the end. A reader checking one claim should not have to guess which source
   covers it.
2. **Do not exceed what the source says.** If the source describes a criterion for
   one crop or one season, do not present it as general. The most common failure
   here is widening a narrow source to fit a broad question.
3. **When sources disagree, say so and attribute both.** Do not silently pick one.
   Name which is more specific to the user's situation and why — a source about
   the same crop and region outranks a general one.
4. **When the corpus holds nothing on point, say exactly that.** "The corpus
   returned nothing for this query" is honest and useful. Presenting recalled
   general knowledge in the same voice as cited local guidance is not.
5. **When the citation conflicts with the local data, the local data wins for
   local claims.** Professional guidance describes general conditions; the
   station's own measurements describe this place. Report both and make the
   distinction explicit.
6. **Keep the quoted passage short enough to read inline** but long enough to be
   self-contained — a complete criterion or a complete recommendation, not a
   fragment cut mid-condition.
7. **Never manufacture a citation.** If you cannot point at retrieved text, the
   claim is yours, and it must be phrased as a general statement rather than
   attributed to a source that did not say it.
